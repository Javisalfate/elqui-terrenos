What we check before recommending a plot

These are the questions people ask us most, and the ones that prevent the most problems. We check them on site, without rushing, the way we would if we were buying.

  1. 1

    That there's really enough irrigation water

    Hearing that a plot "has an acción de agua" (a share of water rights in an irrigation canal) isn't enough. Water here is shared out in turns along each canal, so we look at how many minutes the plot gets per turn and whether that covers its size: some people buy 5,000 m² with very few minutes and then can't irrigate. We also check that the shares are registered and that the purchase deed includes them.

  2. 2

    Whether the river is nearby

    It's the first thing people ask. We tell you how far the river is and how you get down to it.

  3. 3

    That it isn't prone to flooding, landslides or mudflows

    We look at whether it sits next to a ravine, too close to the riverbed or right up against the hillside. And we ask the neighbors what happened in past rainy years.

  4. 4

    That it has its own rol, or a size that allows it to be sold

    Outside village limits, the law only allows farmland to be divided into lots of 5,000 m² or more. A smaller plot can be sold if it already exists as a separate property, with its own rol and its own entry in the property registry. If what you're offered is "acciones y derechos" (a percentage share of a larger property), you don't own your piece: you become a co-owner of the whole thing.

  5. 5

    Access and power

    How you get there, what shape the road is in, and whether there's power on the plot or how far away the nearest connection is.

We check, and we tell you honestly what we find, but this isn't legal advice. Before signing, we recommend having a lawyer do a title search (estudio de títulos) on the plot. If technical studies are needed too, we'll tell you.

Frequently asked questions

If this is your first plot in the valley

What does it mean when a plot "has an acción de agua"? Is that enough?

In the Valle del Elqui, irrigation water arrives through canals, and the right to use it is measured in shares (acciones). Within each canal, water is shared out in turns: each plot gets to irrigate for a set time (minutes or hours) every so many days.

So knowing it "has a share" isn't enough. Three things need checking:

  • How long its turn is, and whether that's enough for the area you want to irrigate.
  • That the shares are recorded in the property registry (Conservador de Bienes Raíces) and in the national water rights register (Catastro Público de Aguas, run by the DGA). Chile's 2022 Water Code reform (Law 21.435) requires them to be registered, and rights that aren't registered in time can lapse.
  • That the purchase deed expressly includes the water shares.

The amount of water also changes from year to year. In droughts, the Río Elqui water board (Junta de Vigilancia) delivers less to the canals: in 2021, canals in Paihuano were receiving around 25% of their usual flow.

Can I buy a plot smaller than 5,000 m²?

It depends on whether that plot already exists legally as a separate property. Outside village limits, Chilean law (Decree Law 3.516) only allows rural land to be divided into lots of at least 5,000 m², certified by the Agriculture and Livestock Service (SAG) and recorded in the property registry.

A smaller plot can be bought if it already has its own rol (property tax ID) and its own entry in the registry, for example because it was divided before that rule existed, or because its title was formalized later. What's best avoided is buying "acciones y derechos" in a larger property: that doesn't make you the owner of your piece, but a co-owner of the whole property alongside the other buyers.

That's why we tell you, for every plot, whether it has its own rol. To confirm its legal status, the right step is a title search by a lawyer.

How can I tell if a plot is at risk of mudflows or flooding?

Some signs are visible on site: whether it sits next to a ravine, very close to the riverbed or at the foot of a hill. It also helps to ask the neighbors what happened in past rainy years. We look at all this during the visit and tell you what we see.

You can also ask the municipal building department (Dirección de Obras) in Paihuano or Vicuña, depending on where the plot is. Zoning plans can mark hazard areas for mudflows, rockfalls or flooding, where building is restricted. If the plot calls for it, we'll suggest getting a specialist's opinion.

Can I buy from abroad or from another part of Chile?

Yes. Many people who buy in the valley live in Santiago, in another region or outside Chile. We can move forward remotely with photos, videos and video calls from the plot [TO BE CONFIRMED], and arrange a visit for when you can come.

If you live outside Chile, you'll need a RUT (Chilean tax ID number) before buying. The tax office (SII) issues it online, but you must first appoint a representative who is domiciled and resident in Chile, through a notarized power of attorney or an advanced electronic signature. If you can't be there for the signing, you can also buy through a representative with power of attorney, prepared by a notary or lawyer. We help you coordinate it.

If you're from Argentina, Bolivia or Peru: the municipality of Paihuano (which includes Monte Grande, Pisco Elqui, Horcón and Alcohuaz) and part of Vicuña are a border zone [TO BE CONFIRMED: check with DIFROL]. Chilean law (Decree Law 1.939) doesn't allow nationals of neighboring countries to buy land in border zones without a special authorization by presidential decree. Check with a lawyer before going ahead.
